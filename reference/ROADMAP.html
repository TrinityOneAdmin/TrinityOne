The one rule that orders everything below

Never describe a protection as more than it is. An honest limit survives a police visit; an overclaim gets someone charged. Where a task exists to make a claim true, it outranks a task that makes the product nicer.

Doing next Top priority

Found by walking the product as a real church would, 2026-07-28 onwards — not by any audit. The first three are the queue: a child who cannot get in, a console that shows nobody’s face, and one PIN prompt too many. They are what a congregation meets on day one.

  • Give a relay an explicit MODE — because “no churches” currently means three different thingsPilot
    An empty relay is open: accept() begins if (!CHURCH_PUBS.size) return true, and the media gate does the same. So removing the last church silently turns a gated relay into one anyone on the internet can write to — which is why the console refuses to do it (“that is the only church on this relay”). A church therefore cannot leave a relay it no longer trusts, and an operator cannot decommission one cleanly.
    Not policy drift — state drift. Public vs private already governs the second and subsequent churches exactly as intended. What is unhandled is the empty state, which today answers three unrelated questions at once: a generic relay (the original standalone product, before a console was attached), a church relay not set up yet, and a church relay deliberately emptied. The first should accept, the second exactly once, the third never.
    Why now: a wiped relay sits open on a public address until someone registers, and the first key to arrive claims it. It also blocks self-service purge — a church erasing itself from a single-church relay is this case.
    Full write-up, with the measurements and the open questions: reference/ROADMAP-NOTES.md §7.
  • Design what a delegated steward actually sees — and give the card real permissionsPilot
    Pilot-critical: the pilot church has four elders who all need access. Copying the church key four times would be the worst possible answer — four devices with full, irreversible authority, any one of which opens every sealed name in the congregation. Delegation is the right mechanism and it has never been designed.
    Getting IN already works. "Help run another church" in the steward app hands the owner a code, they approve it, done. The gap is everything after.
    What a delegate sees today: a near-identical console, a small STEWARD badge, and "Acting as steward · tap to switch". Nothing says what they may do. Zero owner-only controls are disabled — every one looks live and fails when pressed, and until 2026-07-28 most failed SILENTLY: the dialog closed, the toggle stayed put, the giving field showed "Saved ✓", and nothing had happened. A delegate learns the boundaries by hitting them.
    So: orient them once on arrival (what you can do, what only the owner can, that you can be removed at any time); make owner-only controls visibly unavailable with a reason rather than present-then-refused; and have somebody walk the whole console AS a delegate, which nobody ever has — two of the most serious bugs found on 2026-07-28 were in delegated paths precisely because nobody goes there.

    PERMISSIONS ARE A RELAY CHANGE, NOT A UI CHANGE. The steward roster is a flat {pubkeys:[…]} list and the relay's steward check is BINARY — you are a steward or you are not. So per-elder settings ("can post", "can manage members", "can see Finance", "can see Care") need the roster document to carry capabilities AND the relay to enforce them. Hiding buttons alone would be theatre: a delegate could do the hidden thing from any other client, and we would have promised a control that does not exist. Sensible first split — the pastoral and financial ones (Finance, Care, safeguarding) are what a church actually wants to scope; posting and rotas rarely need restricting.
  • A child cannot get in by the route their parent is told to useCopy is wrong now
    Creating a child account shows the parent a QR and says "On your child's phone, open TrinityOne's camera and scan this — it signs them in as…". There is no such camera on a fresh phone. Found 2026-07-28; this path had never been walked.
    Partly done 2026-08-19. The original complaint (“open TrinityOne’s camera” — no such camera exists on a fresh install) was fixed on 2026-07-28. What remained underneath it was worse: for a child whose phone ALREADY has the app — the ordinary case when a church onboards a family — the parent was told to transcribe twelve words onto the second phone, while the restore screen’s “Someone set this up for me” could read the code already on their screen. Proven by driving inviteUrlFor() and seedFromScan() against each other: the scanner accepts that QR exactly. The hand-off copy and the help doc now lead with that route; the camera route stays for a phone with no app yet, and says plainly that it lands in the browser. Still open: the deep link. The QR points at “/” and the app claims only “/join”, so the phone’s camera opens the browser even when the app is installed — and claiming “/” would make a crafted #invite= link one tap from identity replacement inside the native app, which AUDIT-2026-07-25 deliberately refused. That is a security decision, not a copy fix. Held by scripts/child-handoff-route.test.mjs.
    Why it cannot work as built: the child's identity is minted on the PARENT'S phone, and the parent's app never becomes the child — so the transfer flow is no help, because it moves whichever identity is currently active. And the only scanner a fresh install offers belongs to that transfer flow, which expects a live mutually-verified exchange with another running device. A child's phone therefore sits showing its own code, waiting for a partner that never arrives.
    The 12 words do work — "I have my 12 words", typed from the parent's reveal screen — so this is a broken shortcut rather than a locked door. But asking a parent to transcribe twelve words onto a child's phone, in a hall, is exactly the moment the product should be at its easiest.
    Three ways out: make the reveal QR something a fresh install can consume (a scan-to-sign-in entry point, distinct from device transfer); or make the child hand-off use the real transfer flow by having the parent temporarily act as the child; or, until either exists, change the copy to say "type the 12 words" — it currently promises something that does not happen. The last one is hours, and should not wait for the others.
  • Steward / handoff / delegate — three different things wearing one nameTomorrow
    "How do I add the church from my workstation to the phone?" — asked by the person who built it, which is the clearest possible signal. The mechanism EXISTS: the console can show a handoff QR (handoffPayload()) and another device can scan it (adoptScanned). It is simply not findable, and the words around it do not distinguish three quite different acts.
    The three, which the UI currently blurs:
    · Handoff / adopt — copy the SAME church key to a second device. Both then hold full church authority. This is what "get my church onto my phone" means, and it is the buried one.
    · Delegate a steward — a second person gets their OWN key with granted, revocable authority. Nothing is copied.
    · Restore — bring a church back from a backup file or phrase, usually onto a replacement.
    The safety difference should drive the wording, not just clarity: handoff DOUBLES the number of devices holding the church key, and that key opens every sealed name in the congregation — so it doubles what one seizure yields. Delegation does not: it can be revoked, and it never puts the church key on the second device. For most "I also want it on my phone" cases delegation is the better answer, and the interface should say so rather than treating them as equivalent menu items.
    Pairs with the child hand-off and the missing sign-in scanner: three separate places where an account or a church cannot reach a second device by the route the screen implies.

    THE PLAN (agreed 2026-07-28, not yet built). The Security screen carries four cards and three of them say "steward". They are named after MECHANISMS; people arrive with INTENTIONS, and that gap is the whole problem — the handoff worked perfectly once found, and the person who built it could not find it.
    · Rename by the question being asked — "Put this church on another device of mine", "Let someone help run this church", "Help run another church", "Get this church back". Same four things, sorted by why you came.
    · Order by weight, not by similarity. Copying the key is the heaviest act on the screen and currently sits first and largest, with delegation as the afterthought. For most "I want it on my phone too" cases delegation is the better answer, and the screen should say so.
    · Make the copy-the-key path look heavier. Not hidden — just not the default-looking button.
    · Rename carefully: a global search-and-replace also hits the body text that REFERS to these cards ("use Delegated stewards below"), leaving the console pointing at cards no longer called that. Half-renamed navigation is worse than consistent-but-confusing navigation. Tried on 2026-07-28 and reverted for exactly this.

    Two questions to answer BEFORE writing any of it:
    1. Is "put it on my own second device" really the same act as "hand the church to another person"? Today they share one button. Same mechanism, completely different consequence — one is convenience, the other is succession. Splitting them may be the biggest single clarity win, at the cost of a card.
    2. Should delegation be the RECOMMENDED route for a leader's own phone? It is safer, but a delegated console cannot do everything an owner can — so if yes, those limits must be visible at the moment of choosing, not discovered later.
  • Reply privately from a group chat
    Tap someone's name or picture in a group and open a direct conversation with them — the way "reply privately" works in WhatsApp.
    Why it matters here more than in an ordinary group chat: the moment someone needs a quiet word is usually the moment a group thread makes it hardest — a prayer request that should not be public, a safeguarding concern, someone who has said something worrying in front of forty people. The alternative today is leaving the conversation, finding them in the directory and starting again, which is enough friction that people simply do not.
    Must respect the safeguarding rules already in place: an adult cannot open a private thread with a child unless they are cleared, and the relay refuses it whatever the UI offers. So the affordance has to be ABSENT where that applies, not merely refused after the tap.
  • Remove the older forced-PIN dialogCarefully
    Two PIN prompts appear when creating a church. The wizard's is better in every way; the first has no confirm and accepts 4 characters.
    Why it was left in: it is currently the only thing guaranteeing a PIN exists at all. Deleting it needs the wizard proven to cover every route into the console first — otherwise a steward reaches the dashboard with the church key sitting unencrypted, which is worse than the duplication. Half an hour with a clear head, not a rushed edit mid-test.
  • The steward console shows no member avatars at all
    Members see each other's avatars fine — symbols in chat and the directory, and real photos where the church allows them. The CONSOLE draws initials everywhere: members list, chat, join requests, care rows. It has never shown a member's face or symbol anywhere.
    It already has the data and discards it. subscribeMembers stores each member's av and a hasPhoto flag from their profile, then every render site uses an initials badge. The avatar component is not even loaded into steward.html, so there is nothing there that could draw one. (There is also a dead Avatar component with no call sites, which suggests someone meant to and stopped.)
    Why it matters more than it looks: an unnamed member collapses to identical grey initials, so the screen where you decide whether to approve someone tells you nothing about them. Honour the existing photo toggle and the children-never rule at every site, and fall back to the symbol avatar every member already has.
    Worth deciding at the same time: pictures sit in the PUBLIC profile in the clear, while names are now sealed to the congregation. A face identifies a person more surely than a name does. Making photos more visible in the product is fine; leaving them as the one identifying thing still in the clear is the inconsistency to close.
    • Members list in the steward console
    • The member app's people directory and chat
    • Honour the photo toggle + children-never rule in every place it appears
    • Decide whether pictures should be sealed like names
  • Individual QR invites, one per personWell after pilot
    Today a church has ONE joining code. It works, and the poster and bulk slips are already built — but every copy of it is the same key, and it never expires.
    What individual codes buy that a shared one cannot:
    · You know who joined. Right now a join request arrives from a stranger and the steward matches it to a person by name alone. An invite handed to Margaret that comes back as Margaret is a far better signal — and it is exactly the moment safeguarding decisions get made.
    · It can be revoked. One code passed around a WhatsApp group cannot be un-shared without changing it for the whole church. An individual code is revoked alone.
    · It can pre-approve. Now that a new church gates joins by default, a code the steward personally handed over could admit that person without a second approval step — the approval already happened, in the room, on paper.
    · It can be one-use, or expire — so a slip found in a bin months later is worthless.
    The physical workflow is the point: a sheet of slips, one handed to each person after a service. That is how a congregation actually migrates, and the printing side already exists for the poster and bulk slips. Shares its plumbing with printable QR recovery above.
  • Never wipe the member's name before its sealed copy existsData loss
    A locked boot wipes cached identity from disk. Everything in a profile survives on the relay — avatar, picture, about — except the name, which is deliberately kept off it.
    Seen for real on 2026-07-28: a member set "Testi Bob", the sealed copy failed to publish (since fixed), a locked boot wiped the local copy, and the name was simply gone — while the avatar came back untouched, because avatars are public and names are not. Sealing the name removed its only redundancy, so the publish has to be confirmed before the wipe can be safe. The wipe itself is right and should stay.

Before anything ships Now

Everything from the last three days is merged and passing 471 automated tests, and none of it has been run on a phone. That is the only thing between here and a pilot.

  • Work the device checklist end to endBlocks all
    The existing checklist covers the recovery work but predates the name encryption. It needs a second half.
    Why it can't be compressed: the last two days found twelve faults that looked perfect on paper, and the worst one — the roster blanking people's names — was found by reading code, not by any test.
    • Names appear: in chat, the member directory, the steward console
    • Someone awaiting approval shows a readable name, not gibberish
    • Set up a child account — the leader sees the child's name
    • Block a member — everyone else's names still work, history intact
    • Restore from 12 words — name and churches both come back
    • Phone-to-phone transfer, both handsets present
    • A child's app knows it is a child (the clearance fix)
    • Airplane mode: reload the app, it still opens
  • Test on the phone your audience actually hasUnusual
    A cheap Android five years old, throttled to 2G, with the screen in a language you don't read.
    Why: every device test so far has been on a Pixel 10 Pro on wifi. That is not the target device and never was. A shared family phone is also worth one pass — the app assumes one person per handset.
  • Turn off web debugging, and prove it
    webContentsDebuggingEnabled is true in two files. It stays on while you test, and comes off in the same pass as the deploy.
    Why "prove it": a memory note once recorded this as done when it wasn't. Verify by attaching a debugger to the release build and failing to connect.
  • Seize your own phoneUnusual
    Take a test handset with a real church on it, and examine it the way a competent examiner would. Write down everything learned.
    Why: we make claims about what a locked app reveals. This is the only way to know whether they are true. Whatever it finds becomes the honest wording — and one already-known answer is that the app's presence and church membership are visible to a proper inspection.
  • Deploy both halves together
    The relay update and a fresh signed app build. They must go out as a pair.
    Why paired: the console and app now share a secret older versions don't understand. Deploying one without the other blanks names or empties group chats.
    • Wipe test data after the merge, before anyone joins
    • Rebuild + release-sign the app
    • Update the relay box
    • Fresh install against the live system, as a stranger would
    • Confirm the internal-docs leak is closed on the deployed box
  • Confirm the PIN when it is first setBefore pilot
    Type it twice, church creation and member setup both. Noticed while creating a church on 2026-07-28.
    Why it is a bug, not a nicety: the PIN cannot be reset — the app says so itself, "not even by us". So one mistyped digit at setup makes the church key unrecoverable, and with it every sealed name and record that key opens. Data loss caused by a missing keystroke.
  • Answer whether the PIN can be attacked offline, then pick its length
    Proposed 4 → 6 digits. Decide it on the threat, not the digit count.
    The question that settles it: can someone holding the device guess against the stored key directly, or must every attempt go through the app? If it must go through the app, four digits plus a lockout is fine — that is how phones work. If it can be attacked offline, six digits is only 100× harder and still weak; what helps is making each guess SLOW (a deliberately expensive key derivation). A quick look found no such derivation in the identity module, which is a hint rather than a finding — confirm before choosing. Adding two digits without checking could feel safer while changing very little. Pairs with the throttle already on the security list.
  • Get the release signing key off this machine
    If the keystore is lost, no existing install can ever be updated again. Not by us, not by anyone.
    Why now: this is the one failure with no recovery path at all, and it gets worse with every phone that installs the app. Two copies, two places, at least one offline.

Running the pilot Pilot

Two or three churches — enough to break assumptions that only hold for one congregation. Different sizes, different leadership, at least one where you are not in the room.

  • Write the honest limits into the joining packNo code
    Plain words: what this protects you from, and what it does not.
    Why it's a pilot task and not a nicety: a member who understands the limits makes better decisions than one trusting a claim we can't back — and someone in that room may end up explaining it to an officer. Must say plainly that a PIN does not defeat a court order.
  • Run a "bad week" drill with each churchUnusual
    Sit down and rehearse: the bank account is frozen; a steward's laptop is taken; the relay box is seized. What happens in the next hour, and who does what?
    Why: this is the product's entire purpose and it has never been exercised. A drill will find missing tools faster than any feature list — and it tells you which roadmap items below are real and which are theatre.
  • Try to enumerate the pilot churches from outsideUnusual
    Given only the public site and a relay address, how much can an outsider learn — which churches exist, how many members, who they are?
    Why: "unenumerable" is the claim the name encryption exists to support. Success means finding nothing. Do it before real congregations depend on it, and repeat it after every change to what the relay serves — that is exactly how the internal-docs leak was found.
  • Answer the data-protection question before a church asks itNo code
    A UK church using this is a data controller. Members have rights over their data — access, correction, deletion — and the church has retention duties, especially around children.
    Why it's awkward and worth solving: encryption helps enormously — the relay holds ciphertext — but "we can't read it" is not the same as "we have no obligations". A one-page answer a church trustee can hand to their safeguarding officer removes a real adoption blocker.
    • One page a trustee can hand to their safeguarding officer
    • Build "remove this church from this relay" — scoped to one church, backup first, type the church name to confirm, operator only, logged
    • Answer plainly: what happens to our data if we stop using TrinityOne?
  • A church's data must be removable without a shellUnusual
    Wiping a relay by hand on 2026-07-28 took a dozen commands, tripped over file permissions and shell globbing twice, and an earlier auto-detecting version would have reported success while deleting nothing.
    Scope it to ONE church, not the box. A remote endpoint that destroys everything makes the admin token the only thing standing between a congregation and oblivion — a nasty thing to leave on a public relay. Scoped removal is the version that is actually needed in production: a church moving to its own relay, a pilot church leaving, or a lawful erasure request. It is also the honest answer to "what happens to our data if we stop?", which someone will ask.
    Backup first — today proved why — and make them type the church's name rather than click once.
  • Get a UK lawyer's read on steward exposureNo code
    Specifically: what a steward can be compelled to produce, and what happens if they genuinely cannot.
    Why: we are asking volunteers to hold a key that opens their congregation. They deserve to know their position before they accept it, and the answer shapes the multisig design.
  • Decide how a church reaches you when it breaks
    One route, stated in the app, that works when the app is the thing that's broken.
    Why: with three churches this is a phone number. It is worth writing down anyway, because the answer at thirty churches is different and you'll want to know when it changed.
  • Capture what people actually do, without instrumenting themUnusual
    No analytics, no telemetry, no phone-home. Ask instead: a short call at week one and week four with each church.
    Why this constraint is a feature: a product whose whole pitch is "we can't see your data" cannot ship usage tracking. Talking to three churches is better research than a dashboard anyway — and it stays honest at thirty.
  • Write the pre-mortemUnusual
    One page: it is three months from now and the pilot failed. What happened?
    Why: the likely answers ("names showed as Anonymous and it looked broken", "the steward lost the key", "nobody could get a Lightning address") are all cheap to prevent now and expensive to fix in front of a congregation.

Making one target into many After pilot

The strategy is to make attacking churches one-by-one uneconomic. That is an infrastructure and governance problem more than a code problem.

  • Public relays in more than one jurisdiction
    Additional boxes like the current one, run in countries whose legal systems do not readily cooperate with each other.
    Why "jurisdiction" not "location": three servers in three data centres under one legal regime is one demand, not three. The property you want is that no single court order reaches them all. Pick on legal diversity first and latency second.
    • Choose jurisdictions on legal grounds, written down
    • Stand up the first additional public relay
    • Prove a church survives losing its primary relay — pull the plug and watch
    • Confirm operators are separate people, not all you
  • A warrant canary on every public relayUnusual
    A signed statement, refreshed on a schedule, saying no demand has been received. Its absence is the signal.
    Why it fits here: operators can be forbidden from disclosing a demand. They cannot generally be compelled to keep signing something untrue. Cheap to build, and it makes the multi-jurisdiction spread legible to churches rather than a claim they take on faith.
  • Prove the app installs with no app store and no internetUnusual
    From a memory card, a local box, or one phone to another in the room.
    Why: app stores are a single point of removal — the tidiest way to delete this product from a country is one takedown request. The QR identity transfer already proves the pattern works; this extends it to the app itself. It is also how it reaches somewhere with no data.
  • The paper fallback packUnusual
    What a church can print and keep in a drawer: join slips (built), key recovery sheets, and a one-page "the internet is off, what still works" card.
    Why: paper does not have a battery, cannot be remotely wiped, and is not seized as a data-bearing device in the same way. For a congregation, it is also the format their least technical member is least frightened of.
    • Printable QR recovery — scan instead of typing twelve words
    • Encrypt it under a passphrase, so a photographed sheet is not an account
    • One QR per Shamir share — three sheets, three people, any two restore
    • Join slips (built) and the "internet is off" card
  • Recovery you can print and scanParts already built
    Twelve words typed on a phone keyboard is the single most error-prone moment in the product, and it happens when someone has already lost something.
    Nearly all of this is shipped already: qrSVG renders codes today (invites, invoices), splitMnemonic/combineMnemonic do Shamir splitting, and argon2Raw is there for passphrase encryption. What is missing is the printable sheet that puts them together.
    The decision that matters: a bare seed QR is a bearer credential — a camera lifts it silently across a room, which is worse than written words that at least have to be read and copied. So the printed sheet should be encrypted under a passphrase the person keeps separately, or split into shares held by different people. Both are already possible; a plain seed QR should not be the default, however convenient it looks.
    Pairs naturally with the child-account flow, which already shows a login QR — and with the church key work, where printed shares are the physical form of splitting it.
  • Answer the bus questionUnusual
    If this dev box dies, or you are unavailable for six months, what happens to the churches already running?
    Why it belongs on a roadmap: churches are being asked to move their whole community onto this. The honest answer today is that they keep running but stop receiving updates, and the signing key is the pinch point. Write that down and decide whether it is acceptable.

The two big builds after the pilot After pilot

Both have written plans on branches. Both are answers to "what happens when they go around the encryption instead of through it".

  • Split the church key — reference/MULTISIG-CHURCH-KEY.md
    Encrypting names moved the roster's readability rather than removing it: from the relay box to one steward's laptop. Splitting the key means a demand needs several people in several places.
    The risk to respect: lose a quorum of pieces and that church's records are unopenable by anyone, for ever. That is the feature working. It is why nothing is destroyed until a real reassembly has been rehearsed — and why it must not be in the pilot.
    • Decide how pieces reach holders (paper / scan / locked to their account)
    • Decide whether a holder must be a TrinityOne user
    • Confirm there is no escape hatch — any recovery route can also be compelled
    • Build phases A–E
  • Lightning giving — reference/PAYMENTS-LIGHTNING.md
    Not how a UK church gets funded — congregations don't hold bitcoin. It is how a church still receives money the week its bank account is closed over its beliefs.
    The blocker: a church has nowhere to get a Lightning address, and nothing else can be tested until one does. The option that fits the thesis is the church's own relay box issuing it — no account for anyone to close. Same move as making that box the push carrier.
    • Answer the address question — this gates everything

Both are "TrinityOne for something that isn't a single congregation", and both are mostly a language and permissions problem rather than a new product.

  • Keep the direction of the relationship — a church declares its networkDon't break
    Today a church publishes "we belong to X". A network cannot claim a church.
    Why this matters more than it looks: it is the answer to "can a denomination watch its churches?" — no, unless invited, and the invitation is revocable by the church alone. Any oversight feature that quietly reverses this direction turns the product into the thing it was built against. Whatever gets built below, this stays.
  • Design what a network console is actually for
    The identity switcher and network view exist; there is no network dashboard behind them. Before building one, decide whether the model is oversight or support.
    The design question: Regions Beyond over Trinity Littlehampton is an apostolic sphere, not a head office. That argues for a console that resources — shares a safeguarding policy, a teaching series, a rota template, a trusted relay — rather than one that reports. A dashboard of attendance and giving across member churches is the obvious build and probably the wrong one; it also creates exactly the aggregated target this product avoids.
    • Ask two real network leaders what they actually need, before designing
    • Default: a network sees no individual member of any church, ever
    • Share downward: policies, series, templates, a relay churches can trust
    • If any aggregate is shown, the church opts in per figure and can see what it shares
    • Leaving a network is one tap and needs nobody's permission
  • Handle the ordinary messiness: a church in more than one network
    A congregation may sit under a denomination, a local churches-together group, and a mission partnership at once — and a leader may serve several churches.
    Why to check early: the member side had exactly this bug last week — a person in two churches went nameless in one because the relay kept a single slot. The same single-slot assumption is worth hunting for on the network side before anyone hits it.
  • Make the vocabulary switchable
    "Church", "congregation", "steward", "member", "giving" are written into every screen. A foodbank has volunteers and donors; a charity has trustees and supporters.
    Why it is worth doing properly: an organisation that reads "your church" on every screen concludes the product is not for them within a minute, whatever the feature list says. This is a labelling layer chosen at setup, not a fork — and it is the cheapest way to widen who this serves without diluting what it is.
  • Map the tools onto how a charity actually works
    Much of it already fits — Finance models restricted and designated funds today, which is the hard part and the thing generic tools get wrong.
    Where it fits and where it doesn't: teams map to projects, rotas to volunteer shifts, safeguarding is if anything more demanding. What is missing is trustee-shaped: who is a trustee, an annual return, a reserves policy, and Gift Aid — the same Gift Aid work the payments plan needs, which makes it one job serving two audiences.
    • Trustees as a distinct role, not just "stewards"
    • An export a trustee can take to an accountant or the regulator
    • Gift Aid — shared with the payments plan, build once
    • Check safeguarding still holds when there is no congregation, only volunteers
  • Notice that adjacent organisations are often the ones under most pressureUnusual
    A street-preaching ministry, a pregnancy counselling service, a group supporting converts from other faiths — these attract attention a Sunday congregation does not.
    Why this belongs on the roadmap and not just in marketing: if the hardest cases are adjacent organisations rather than churches, then serving them is not a widening of scope — it is the core use case arriving under a different name, and their requirements should shape the product rather than be retrofitted to it.

Deliberately not doing Standing

As useful as the task list. Each of these will be suggested by someone reasonable.

  • Usage analytics of any kind
    A product whose pitch is "we can't see your data" cannot ship telemetry. Talk to churches instead.
  • Holding anyone's money, even briefly
    No balance, no float, no custody, for any convenience gain.
  • A central directory of churches
    It is the target list. Discovery has to work without one.
  • Any recovery route we control
    If we can restore a church's data, we can be compelled to.
  • Naming a protection more strongly than it deserves
    The standing rule at the top of this page.
